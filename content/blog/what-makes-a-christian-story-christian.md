Put a pastor in the story.

Add a church scene.

Let somebody quote a Bible verse.

Play worship music during the emotional moment.

Is the film Christian now?

The question matters because Christian media is much bigger than the presence of recognisable Christian symbols.

A story can mention God frequently while saying very little that is truthful about Him.

Another story may contain no sermon at all while exploring forgiveness, sacrifice, sin, justice, reconciliation or hope with unusual depth.

So what actually makes a Christian story Christian?

There may not be one simple formula, but there are important questions worth asking.

## The Gospel cannot simply be decoration

Christianity should not function like a label placed on a story after everything else has been created.

If removing the church scenes, Christian music and Bible references leaves the meaning of the story completely unchanged, then those elements may only be decoration.

A Gospel-centred story should be shaped at a deeper level.

How does it understand human beings?

What does it believe is wrong with us?

Where does it locate hope?

What does forgiveness cost?

Does grace mean that choices no longer have consequences?

What does restoration look like?

What kind of life is ultimately presented as good?

Stories answer questions like these whether the filmmaker intends to or not.

A Christian storyteller therefore has to think beyond representation and into worldview.

## Christian characters should still be human characters

There is another danger.

Sometimes Christian storytelling becomes so concerned with delivering the correct lesson that its characters stop behaving like recognisable people.

Everybody knows the right thing to say.

Doubt disappears quickly.

Conflicts are resolved neatly.

The Christian character never struggles with pride, fear, envy, temptation or misunderstanding.

But Scripture itself does not tell stories that way.

Its people are often complicated.

Peter can confess that Jesus is the Christ and later deny knowing Him.

David can demonstrate remarkable faith and commit terrible sin.

Thomas can follow Jesus and still struggle to believe the testimony of the resurrection.

The Bible's honesty about people does not weaken its message.

It makes grace easier to understand.

Christian stories should be able to show believers as people still being formed.

## Excellence matters because the story matters

A good message does not remove the need for good filmmaking.

Writing matters.

Acting matters.

Cinematography matters.

Sound matters.

Editing matters.

Pacing matters.

Production design matters.

The audience should not be expected to excuse weak craft simply because the creators had sincere intentions.

FaithCine's own commitment to serious craft comes from this conviction: Christian media should care deeply about both truth and the quality of the experience carrying that truth.

The Gospel does not need entertainment to become powerful.

But when Christians choose film, animation, audio, design or another creative form, they should respect the form enough to learn how to use it well.

## Not every Christian story requires a sermon

Some stories will proclaim the Gospel explicitly.

That is good.

Others may explore one part of reality illuminated by a Christian understanding of the world.

A marriage being rebuilt after betrayal.

A father learning to ask his child for forgiveness.

A young person deciding whether success is worth becoming someone they no longer recognise.

A family grieving while refusing to pretend that faith removes pain.

A community choosing mercy when revenge would be easier.

These stories can create space for Christian truth without turning every character into a preacher.

Jesus Himself frequently taught through stories.

A story gives people room to see.

## Hope should be deeper than a happy ending

Christian storytelling should also be careful about confusing hope with ease.

Sometimes the business does not recover.

The relationship is not restored.

The illness does not disappear.

The person who did wrong never apologises.

Faith does not guarantee that every story resolves exactly as its characters desire.

Christian hope is deeper than circumstances arranging themselves pleasantly before the credits.

It is rooted finally in who God is, what Christ has done and the promise that suffering and death do not have the final word.

That makes room for Christian stories that are joyful.

It also makes room for stories that are painful, unresolved and quiet.

Hope can survive both.

## The question is not only “Is it clean?”

Families understandably care about what enters their homes.

Content choices matter.

But Christian engagement with media cannot stop at asking whether a film contains profanity, sexuality or violence.

A technically “clean” story can still celebrate greed, vanity, cruelty or self-worship.

Another story may portray brokenness honestly in order to expose its consequences.

Discernment asks deeper questions.

What does this story love?

What does it make admirable?

What does it ask me to laugh at?

What does it tell me a successful human life looks like?

What kind of person does it encourage me to become?

Those questions matter for audiences.

They matter even more for Christian creators.

## We need more than Christian versions of everything

Christian media does not need to imitate every successful format in popular culture and attach a Bible verse to it.

There is room for something more imaginative.

Stories rooted in our own communities.

African Christian experiences told with care.

Children seeing faith presented with intelligence rather than condescension.

Documentaries that take difficult questions seriously.

Films about ordinary believers whose lives may never look spectacular but whose faithfulness matters.

Work that can be unmistakably Christian without becoming shallow.

That is part of the larger vision behind FaithCine Stories.

The project is still in early development. But the aim is clear: Christian films, shorts, series and documentaries centred on the Gospel and the realities of faith, family, hope and restoration.

Not Christian content simply because the label says so.

Stories shaped by truth.

Stories made with serious craft.

Stories that understand people.

Stories that leave room for grace.

Stories that, in one way or another, help people see Jesus.
